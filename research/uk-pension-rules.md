## 1. State Pension

- **Full new State Pension: £241.30/week (≈£12,547.60/year)** for 2026/27. Confirmed directly from gov.uk. The annual figure is my calculation (£241.30 × 52) — gov.uk's page did not itself state the annual total, but this is the standard convention used across secondary sources (Moorepay, ukpensionplanner.co.uk) which independently corroborate £241.30/week and £12,547/year for 2026/27, driven by the April 2026 4.8% triple-lock uprating.
  Source: [gov.uk – New State Pension: what you'll get](https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension/what-youll-get) (current, 2026/27); corroborating: [Moorepay – full State Pension amount 2026-27](https://www.moorepay.co.uk/payroll-hr-rates/what-is-the-new-full-state-pension-amount/)
- **Qualifying years**: 35 qualifying NI years needed for the full new State Pension (if your NI record started after April 2016). A minimum of 10 qualifying years is generally needed to get *any* new State Pension — this 10-year figure is well-established, stable government guidance but was not visible in the specific gov.uk excerpt fetched this session. **[Moderately confident, standard/stable rule — recommend a final direct-fetch confirmation from gov.uk before publishing]**
  Source: [gov.uk – New State Pension: what you'll get](https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension/what-youll-get)
- **State Pension age**: currently rising from 66 to 67, phased between 6 May 2026 and 6 April 2028 (affects those born 6 April 1960 – 5 April 1977). A further rise to 68 is currently legislated for 2044–2046, but the government launched a review in 2025 into whether this should be brought forward — no decision has been made as of August 2026. **Calculator implication: for anyone under ~50 today, don't hard-code SPA=67; flag that it may rise further.**
  Source: [Which? – Will the state pension age rise to 68 sooner than planned?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/will-the-state-pension-age-rise-to-68-sooner-than-planned-aJNpY0H1VeoS); [House of Commons Library – State Pension age review](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/sn06546/)
- **Checking your NI record / buying back years**: Check via the HMRC app or your Personal Tax Account (gov.uk). You can normally only pay voluntary Class 3 NI contributions for the past 6 tax years (deadline 5 April each year). Note: an extended one-off deadline (5 April 2025) allowed buying back gaps as far back as 2006–2018; that extended window has now closed, so from 2025/26 onwards it's back to the standard 6-year rolling window. Always check your personal forecast first, since buying back years does **not** always increase your pension (e.g. if you were contracted out).
  Source: [MoneyHelper – Voluntary NI contributions and the State Pension](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/state-pension/voluntary-national-insurance-contributions-and-the-state-pension); [gov.uk – Voluntary National Insurance](https://www.gov.uk/voluntary-national-insurance-contributions); [gov.uk news – taxpayers given more time for voluntary NI](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/taxpayers-given-more-time-for-voluntary-national-insurance-contributions)

## 2. Tax relief on contributions

- **Annual Allowance (AA): £60,000 for 2026/27** — confirmed directly from gov.uk ("This is £60,000 this tax year"). This is the cap on total pension input (personal + employer contributions combined) that can receive tax relief / be tax-free of the Annual Allowance charge in a tax year.
  Source: [gov.uk – Tax on your private pension contributions: Annual allowance](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-your-private-pension/annual-allowance)
- **Tapered Annual Allowance**: only relevant above ~£200k+ income (unlikely for this user, but worth documenting for completeness). Taper applies when *both* threshold income > £200,000 AND adjusted income > £260,000 in the same tax year. Above those thresholds the £60,000 AA reduces by £1 for every £2 of adjusted income over £260,000, down to a minimum AA of £10,000.
  Source: [MoneyHelper – Tapered annual allowance explained](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/tax-and-pensions/tapered-annual-allowance); [pension-helper.co.uk – Pension Annual Allowance 2026/27](https://pension-helper.co.uk/guides/pension-advice/pension-annual-allowance-2026-27/)
- **Money Purchase Annual Allowance (MPAA): £10,000/year**, confirmed for the current period. This replaces the standard £60k AA once you've *flexibly accessed* a defined contribution pension (e.g. taken income via drawdown or UFPLS — but not simply taking the 25% tax-free lump sum on its own, which does not trigger it). Relevant to flag if she starts drawing from Aviva/People's Pension early while still contributing.
  Source: [Royal London for advisers – Money purchase annual allowance (MPAA)](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/contributions-and-tax-relief/money-purchase-annual-allowance/); note one source (mypensionexpert) quoted this specifically for 2025/26 — the £10,000 figure has been stable since April 2023 and multiple 2026-dated sources confirm no change for 2026/27.
- **Carry forward**: you can use unused Annual Allowance from the previous 3 tax years, on top of the current year's £60,000 — in principle up to £240,000 in a single year (£60k + up to £180k carried forward), provided you have enough relevant UK earnings (for personal contributions) or, for employer contributions, provided the company can justify it (see section 3).
  Source: [pension-helper.co.uk – Pension Annual Allowance 2026/27](https://pension-helper.co.uk/guides/pension-advice/pension-annual-allowance-2026-27/); [Royal London for advisers – Tax Relief and Annual Allowance](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/contributions-and-tax-relief/)
- **100%-of-relevant-UK-earnings cap on personal contributions**: tax relief on *personal* contributions is capped at the lower of the Annual Allowance and 100% of your relevant UK earnings for the year. E.g. earnings of £30,000 → tax relief available on at most £30,000 of personal contributions that tax year, even though the AA is £60,000. This does **not** apply to employer contributions (see section 3) — important for a self-employed/director user comparing personal vs company contributions.
  Source: [litrg.org.uk – Tax relief on pension contributions](https://www.litrg.org.uk/pensions/paying-pensions/tax-relief-pension-contributions)
- **£3,600 gross limit for non-earners**: anyone with no earnings (or earnings under £3,600) can still get basic-rate tax relief on contributions up to £3,600 gross per year — i.e. pay in £2,880 net and HMRC tops it up with £720 basic-rate relief, until age 75. Relevant if she has a low/no-earnings year.
  Source: [Standard Life – Annual allowance questions answered](https://www.standardlife.co.uk/articles/article-page/annual-allowance-questions-answered)
- **Relief at source vs net pay** (mechanics, not scheme-specific figures):
  - *Net pay* schemes deduct contributions from gross salary before tax is calculated — higher/additional-rate relief is automatic and immediate through payroll.
  - *Relief at source* schemes (used by most personal pensions/SIPPs and many workplace DC schemes, including typically People's Pension and Aviva's personal-pension-style products) deduct contributions from *net* (after-tax) pay; the provider then claims back 20% basic-rate relief from HMRC and adds it to the pot. A basic-rate taxpayer ends up in the same place either way. A higher/additional-rate taxpayer using relief at source only gets the 20% added automatically — they must **actively claim the extra 20%/25% via Self Assessment or by contacting HMRC**, it is not automatic. This is a common trap and worth flagging explicitly in the calculator/content for a self-employed higher-rate year.
  Source: [Royal London for Employers – Tax relief at source](https://employer.royallondon.com/help-resources/help-centre/making-contributions/making-contributions-using-tax-relief-at-source/); [pension-helper.co.uk – Relief at Source vs Net Pay](https://pension-helper.co.uk/guides/pension-advice/relief-at-source-vs-net-pay/); [MoneyHelper – Pension tax relief](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/tax-and-pensions/tax-relief-and-your-pension)
  - The classic "£80 becomes £100" framing (for a basic-rate taxpayer paying £80 net, HMRC adds £20 basic-rate relief to make a £100 gross contribution) is the standard illustrative example used across the industry for relief at source; this exact number could not be found quoted verbatim on a gov.uk/MoneyHelper page in this session's searches, but it is mathematically exactly what a relief-at-source 20% top-up does (£80 × 1.25 = £100) and is safe to use as illustrative framing. **[Mechanically correct; exact "£80→£100" phrase not independently sourced from an official page this session]**

## 3. Limited company / director contributions — HIGHEST-PRIORITY SECTION for this user

This is the area she's actively chasing her accountant about, so it needs to be concrete and correct.

- **Employer contributions from company funds**: A limited company can pay pension contributions directly for its director(s)/employee(s) out of company funds. These are **not** counted against the director's personal income, and unlike a personal contribution there is **no 100%-of-earnings cap** — meaning a director can have the company pay in up to the Annual Allowance (£60,000, plus carry forward) *even in a year with little or no salary*, as long as the contribution is otherwise justifiable (see "wholly and exclusively" below).
  Source: [getpenfold.com – How much can my company pay into my pension?](https://getpenfold.com/pension-guides/how-much-can-a-company-contribute-to-a-directors-pension); [itcontracting.com – Pension contributions and tax relief for company directors 2026/27](https://www.itcontracting.com/contractor-pensions/pension-contributions-and-tax-relief-for-limited-company-owners/)
- **Corporation tax deductibility & the "wholly and exclusively" rule**: Since 6 April 2006, employers can claim an unlimited Corporation Tax deduction for contributions paid into a registered pension scheme, provided the payment is made **"wholly and exclusively" for the purposes of the trade** (a long-standing general Corporation Tax deductibility test, not pension-specific). HMRC's own manual states that employer pension contributions "will normally pass the wholly and exclusively test and qualify for tax relief," but for controlling directors/shareholder-directors, HMRC may look at whether the contribution is reasonable relative to the work done and overall remuneration package — i.e. don't pay in an amount wildly disproportionate to the work the director actually does for the company.
  Source: [gov.uk HMRC internal manual – BIM46070: wholly & exclusively: payments by third parties](https://gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim46070); [getpenfold.com – Pension Tax Relief for Company Directors](https://getpenfold.com/news/how-pensions-reduce-tax-for-company-directors)
- **Why employer contributions beat salary/dividends for a director with spare company cash**:
  - Paying yourself more salary costs the company employer's NI (currently 15% on earnings above the secondary threshold — outside scope of this pension research, but worth noting as the comparison point) and costs you employee NI + income tax.
  - Paying yourself dividends means the company must first pay Corporation Tax on the profit, then you pay dividend tax personally, with **no Corporation Tax deduction** for the dividend itself.
  - An employer pension contribution instead: (a) reduces the company's Corporation Tax bill (deductible expense), (b) is **not subject to employer or employee National Insurance at all**, and (c) is not subject to income tax when paid in (only later, when eventually drawn as pension income, and even then 25% is tax-free — see section 5). This "no NI, full CT relief" combination is why employer pension contributions are usually the most tax-efficient way to extract surplus profit from a company, especially compared to dividends or salary once basic salary/NI thresholds are already covered.
  Source: [getpenfold.com – How pensions reduce tax for company directors](https://getpenfold.com/news/how-pensions-reduce-tax-for-company-directors); general mechanics corroborated by [Royal London for advisers – Company and employer contributions and tax relief](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/contributions-and-tax-relief/employer-contributions-and-tax-relief/)
- **Employer contributions are bounded by the Annual Allowance, not by personal earnings**: confirmed — "there's no set cap on employer contributions, and the amount isn't linked to the employee's earnings... company contributions don't require you to have £60,000 of personal salary." The only ceiling is the Annual Allowance (£60,000 for 2026/27) plus any carry forward, and the "wholly and exclusively"/reasonableness test above.
  Source: [getpenfold.com – How much can my company pay into my pension?](https://getpenfold.com/pension-guides/how-much-can-a-company-contribute-to-a-directors-pension)
- **Carry forward for employer contributions**: the same 3-year carry-forward mechanism (up to £240,000 total in a year, current £60k + up to £180k from the previous 3 years) is available for employer contributions, provided the director was a member of a registered pension scheme in those earlier years (even if no contributions were actually paid in those years).
  Source: [Royal London for advisers – Tax Relief and Annual Allowance](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/contributions-and-tax-relief/); [M&G Wealth Adviser – Pension Annual Allowance Q&A](https://www.mandg.com/wealth/adviser-services/tech-matters/pensions/annual-allowance/annual-allowance-questions)
- **Salary sacrifice basics**: an employee (including a director) agrees to give up part of their contractual salary/bonus in exchange for the employer paying that amount into their pension instead. Because the sacrificed amount is never "earned" as salary, **both employee and employer NI are saved** on it (this is on top of, and different from, an ordinary employer contribution funded from spare company cash — salary sacrifice is really a way of converting what would have been salary into an employer contribution). Salary cannot be sacrificed below National Minimum/Living Wage (though this restriction doesn't bind most owner-directors who set their own low salary already). Employers can choose to pass their NI saving back into the employee's pension, pay it to the employee, or keep it. **Upcoming change**: from 2029, salary sacrifice NI savings are due to be capped at £2,000/year per employee under new rules — worth flagging as a future change, not current.
  Source: [Penfold – Salary Sacrifice Pensions Explained](https://getpenfold.com/employer-tools/salary-sacrifice-pensions-explained); [labfp.co.uk – Salary Sacrifice Is Changing — But Not for Business Owners (2026)](https://www.labfp.co.uk/post/salary-sacrifice-is-changing-but-not-for-business-owners-here-s-what-directors-need-to-know-in-20)

**Calculator implication**: the single highest-value message for this user is "ask your accountant about the company paying pension contributions directly, rather than paying yourself more salary/dividends and then contributing personally" — she has already done this. The calculator should let her model an employer-contribution scenario distinct from a personal-contribution scenario, since the tax mechanics genuinely differ (no NI, no 100%-earnings cap for employer contributions — but the same £60k AA ceiling applies to the combined total of both).

## 4. Auto-enrolment

- **Minimum contribution: 8% of qualifying earnings total (3% employer + 5% employee, the employee's 5% includes tax relief)**, unchanged and confirmed for 2026/27.
  Source: [Moorepay – Automatic enrolment rates 2026-27](https://www.moorepay.co.uk/payroll-hr-rates/automatic-enrolment/)
- **Qualifying earnings band 2026/27: £6,240 (lower threshold) to £50,270 (upper threshold)** — CONFIRMED via a DWP/parliamentary primary source: all automatic enrolment thresholds for 2026/27 are being held at their 2025/26 levels. Only earnings *within* this band count toward the statutory minimum calculation — e.g. someone earning £30,000 only has 8% applied to (£30,000 − £6,240) = £23,760, not the full salary.
  Source: [Parliament – Review of the Automatic Enrolment Earnings Trigger and Qualifying Earnings Band for 2026/27 (DWP, 18 Dec 2025)](https://data.parliament.uk/DepositedPapers/Files/DEP2025-0866/251218_AE_Thresholds_Review_2026-27.pdf); [Written Ministerial Statement HCWS1206](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2025-12-18/hcws1206)
- **Is 8% enough? — "12–15% of salary" and "half your age" rules of thumb**:
  - The widely-cited **"save 12–15% of salary" (including employer contributions)** guideline is a common industry rule of thumb (found in MoneyWeek, Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Schwab-style consumer guidance), generally pitched at someone starting to save in their 20s/early 30s. It is **not** a government or regulator-mandated figure — 8% is simply the statutory *minimum*, and multiple consumer-finance sources independently converge on "12–15%+ of salary" (or similar 15% figures) as a more realistic target for a comfortable retirement, especially for later starters.
  - The **"half your age when you start, as a percentage of salary"** rule (e.g. start at 30 → aim for 15%; start at age 40 → aim for 20%) is a popular consumer-finance heuristic, again not an official government rule — it's widely repeated across UK personal finance sites (financielle.com and similar). It is a reasonable, honest rule of thumb for the calculator to present as "a well-known guideline" but should **not** be presented as an official government recommendation, since it could not be traced to a DWP/FCA/MoneyHelper source. **[Origin: consumer-finance folk wisdom, not a regulator publication — flag as "rule of thumb" language only, not authoritative]**
  Source: [MoneyWeek – How much should I pay into a pension?](https://moneyweek.com/personal-finance/pensions/how-much-should-i-pay-into-a-pension); [financielle.com – The half-your-age rule explained](https://financielle.com/how-much-should-you-actually-be-paying-into-your-pension/); [T. Rowe Price – savings by age](https://www.troweprice.com/personal-investing/resources/insights/youre-age-35-50-or-60-how-much-should-you-have-by-now.html)

## 5. Access

- **Normal Minimum Pension Age (NMPA)**: currently **55**, rising to **57 on 6 April 2028**. Confirmed. Exceptions apply for firefighters/police/armed forces schemes, and for people who already held an unqualified right to a lower pension age (or were part-way through a qualifying transfer) as of 4 November 2021 — these people can retain a "protected pension age" below 57.
  Source: [Aviva – Changes to the normal minimum pension age](https://www.aviva.co.uk/retirement/pension-basics/changes-to-pension-age/); [LexisNexis UK – NMPA increase to 57 (6 April 2028)](https://www.lexisnexis.com/en-gb/legal/guidance/normal-minimum-pension-age-protected-pension-age)
- **25% tax-free lump sum**: standard entitlement to take up to 25% of a pension pot tax-free (as a Pension Commencement Lump Sum, or via UFPLS — see below), subject to the Lump Sum Allowance cap.
- **Lump Sum Allowance (LSA): £268,275** — confirmed. This caps the *total* tax-free cash you can take across all your pensions over your lifetime (25% of the old £1,073,100 Lifetime Allowance). Any lump sum taken above the LSA is taxed at your marginal income tax rate rather than being tax-free.
  Source: [ReAssure – Lump Sum Allowance and Lump Sum & Death Benefit Allowance](https://www.reassure.co.uk/pensions/lump-sum-allowances/); [Royal London for advisers – Lump sum and lump sum death benefit allowances](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/benefit-options/Lump-sum-and-lump-sum-death-benefit-allowances-from-April-2024/)
- **Lump Sum and Death Benefit Allowance (LSDBA): £1,073,100** — caps total tax-free lump sums *plus* tax-free death-benefit lump sums combined; amounts above this are taxed at the recipient's marginal rate.
  Source: as above.
- **Abolition of the Lifetime Allowance**: the LTA was abolished from 6 April 2024, replaced by the LSA/LSDBA/Overseas Transfer Allowance framework above. There is no longer an overall cap on total pension pot size, or LTA charge on excess growth/income — only these lump-sum-specific caps remain. Given this user's total pot (~£30k plus modest future contributions and possibly home-sale proceeds), the LSA/LSDBA are extremely unlikely to bind, but worth a one-line mention for completeness/reassurance ("you won't run into this").
  Source: [Standard Life – Removal of Lifetime Allowance FAQ](https://www.standardlife.co.uk/lta-faq)
- **Drawdown vs annuity vs UFPLS (plain English)**:
  - **Annuity**: you hand over some or all of your pot to an insurer in exchange for a guaranteed income for life (or a fixed term). No flexibility once bought, no investment risk, income continues even if you live much longer than expected — good for certainty, bad for flexibility or leaving money to family (unless you buy specific guarantees).
  - **Flexi-access drawdown**: your pot stays invested; you can take up to 25% tax-free up front and then draw taxable income as and when you like from the rest. Flexible, but investment risk remains and the pot can run out if withdrawals/market performance are unfavourable.
  - **UFPLS (Uncrystallised Funds Pension Lump Sum)**: take money directly out of an untouched ("uncrystallised") pot in one or more lump sums; each UFPLS payment is automatically 25% tax-free / 75% taxable at the point it's paid, rather than crystallising the whole pot's tax-free entitlement up front. Effectively a lump-sum-by-lump-sum way of accessing money without formally moving into a drawdown arrangement.
  Source: [ii.co.uk – UFPLS Explained](https://www.ii.co.uk/ii-accounts/sipp/ufpls); [amberriver.com – UFPLS vs Drawdown](https://amberriver.com/personal-pensions/ufpls-vs-pension-drawdown/)
- **Current typical annuity rates (2026, illustrative market examples, not personalised quotes)**:
  - For a 65-year-old, **level, single-life** annuities are broadly quoted around **£5,500/year per £100,000** invested in current market commentary (i.e. ~5.5%).
  - **RPI-linked, single-life** annuities for the same profile are broadly **£3,800–£4,500/year per £100,000** (i.e. starting income roughly 35–45% lower than level, in exchange for payments that rise with inflation).
  - These are illustrative market-level figures that change frequently with gilt yields and vary by provider/health/lifestyle underwriting — **do not hard-code these into the calculator as guaranteed rates**; if annuity income is shown, either pull a live quote source or clearly label figures as "illustrative, correct as of August 2026, get a personalised quote before deciding."
  Source: [Retirement Line – Latest Annuity Rates Tables, August 2026](https://www.retirementline.co.uk/annuities/annuity-rates); [pensionbible.co.uk – Annuity Rates UK](https://www.pensionbible.co.uk/guides/annuity-rates-uk) **[Rates are illustrative/aggregated from secondary sources, not fetched from a live annuity-rate API — flag as indicative only]**

## 6. Transfers & consolidation

- **The People's Pension charges**: a management charge of **0.5% of the pot per year**, plus (from 1 April 2026) a flat annual charge of **£6.50** deducted from pots of £106.50 and over. There is also a "savings reward" — a partial rebate of 0.1%–0.3% on the management charge for pots over £3,000, meaning larger pots effectively pay a lower net percentage. All charges sit within the government's charge cap for combination-charge schemes (max 0.6% AMC + up to £10 flat fee equivalent, or equivalent combination structures).
  Source: [thepeoplespension.co.uk – Cost and charges](https://thepeoplespension.co.uk/pension/basics/member-annual-management-charge/transaction-costs/); [peoplespension.co.uk – Member management charge](https://www.peoplespension.co.uk/member-management-charge/)
- **Aviva workplace pension charges**: annual management charge for members typically capped at **0.75%**, but Aviva's own default option (the "My Future Lifestyle" target-date/lifestyling fund) has a much lower total annual fund charge of **0.28%/year**. Passive tracker funds within Aviva schemes can be as low as 0.10–0.15%, while actively managed funds run 0.50–0.75%. (Exact charge depends on her specific scheme/policy — Aviva provides an online charges lookup by policy number.)
  Source: [Drewberry – Aviva Workplace Pension Review 2026](https://www.drewberryinsurance.co.uk/employee-benefits/workplace-pension-scheme/aviva-workplace-pension-review); [pensionbible.co.uk – Aviva Pension Fees, Charges](https://www.pensionbible.co.uk/providers/aviva)
- **Typical modern SIPP platform fees (2026, for context if considering consolidating into a SIPP instead)**:
  - Hargreaves Lansdown: ~0.35% platform fee for pots under £250,000 (recently cut some share-dealing fees from £11.95 to £6.95).
  - AJ Bell: ~0.25% platform fee, capped, plus low-cost dealing (~£1.50/trade quoted by one source).
  - interactive investor: flat monthly fee model (~£4.99–£11.99/month depending on plan) rather than percentage-based — comparatively cheap for **larger** pots but not necessarily for a ~£30k pot, where a 0.25–0.35% percentage-fee platform (AJ Bell/HL) may work out cheaper in cash terms than a flat monthly fee.
  - Vanguard (own-platform, Vanguard funds only): ~0.15% platform fee.
  **Calculator implication**: for her current pot size (~£30k), do the actual arithmetic per platform rather than assuming "flat fee = cheapest" — flat-fee platforms tend to become cheaper only once a pot is large enough (commonly cited crossover is roughly £50k–£100k+ depending on the specific percentage-fee provider).
  Source: [smartinvestoruk.co.uk – Best SIPP Providers UK 2026](https://smartinvestoruk.co.uk/best-sipp-uk/); [Kepler Trust Intelligence – HL slashes ISA and SIPP fees, Jan 2026](https://www.trustintelligence.co.uk/investor/articles/hargreaves-lansdown-slashes-isa-and-sipp-fees-jan-2026) **[Fee figures aggregated from comparison sites, not fetched directly from each provider's own current fee page this session — recommend a direct provider-page check before publishing exact numbers]**
- **What to check before transferring anything (checklist)**:
  1. **Guaranteed Annuity Rates (GARs)** — some older policies carry a contractual right to a fixed, historically very favourable annuity rate; transferring loses this permanently and it may be worth far more than the pot's face value. Check policy documents/provider directly.
  2. **Other safeguarded benefits** — any guarantees, minimum growth rates, or a protected (lower) pension age attached to the policy.
  3. **Exit fees** — some legacy/older-style personal pensions (less common for modern workplace DC schemes like Aviva/People's Pension, but worth checking) charge an exit penalty for transferring out.
  4. **Defined benefit (final salary) pensions valued over £30,000**: by law, if any of the pension being transferred has "safeguarded benefits" (most commonly DB/final salary rights) worth more than £30,000, you are **required** to take regulated financial advice from a suitably FCA-authorised adviser before the transfer can proceed — the receiving/ceding scheme trustees must confirm this advice was taken. Below £30,000 there's no *legal* requirement for the ceding scheme to insist on advice, though FCA rules mean any adviser who does get involved must still be properly authorised regardless of value. (Aviva/People's Pension DC pots are not defined benefit, so this specific rule is unlikely to bite for the ~£30k described, but is essential general knowledge if she has any other older DB entitlement she hasn't mentioned.)
  5. **Employer contribution matching** — check whether staying in a current workplace scheme is tied to ongoing employer contributions that would be lost by transferring out while still employed there.
  Source: [Royal London for advisers – Safeguarded benefits and pension transfer advice](https://adviser.royallondon.com/technical-central/pensions/transfers/safeguarded-benefits/); [ii.co.uk – Pension transfer checklist](https://www.ii.co.uk/pension-transfer-checklist)
- **Pension Tracing Service**: free government service to help locate contact details for old/lost workplace pensions (it identifies the pension administrator for a former employer's scheme; it does **not** tell you whether you have a pension there or its value — you have to contact the scheme yourself once traced).
  Source: [gov.uk – Find pension contact details](https://www.gov.uk/find-pension-contact-details); [MoneyHelper – How to find old or lost pensions](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/pension-problems/tracing-and-finding-lost-pensions)

## 7. Risk / investment choice

- **What a "default fund" is**: the fund a member's contributions go into automatically if they don't make an active investment choice — legally required to be a reasonable default suitable for a broad membership, used by the vast majority of workplace pension savers (including, very likely, both her Aviva and People's Pension pots unless she actively chose otherwise).
- **Lifestyling / target-date glidepaths**: most default funds use "lifestyling," automatically shifting the asset mix from mostly equities toward bonds/cash as the saver approaches their *stated target retirement date/age on file with the scheme* — not their actual chosen retirement date, which is a common source of mismatch if someone hasn't updated their expected retirement age with the provider. This de-risking is designed to reduce the chance of a market crash hitting the pot right before it's needed, at the cost of lower expected growth in the years it's ramping down.
- **Why a 40-something with 20+ years shouldn't likely be in cash/cautious**: with several decades to retirement, the standard finance argument is that short-term volatility matters much less than long-run growth, and that being defensively positioned (cash/bonds) this early sacrifices substantial expected long-term growth for a risk (short-term loss) that isn't relevant on a 20+ year horizon; one commonly cited stat is that equities have historically beaten cash roughly 91% of 10-year holding periods (UK data cited by Barclays-style equity/gilt studies). **This is a directional, historically-grounded argument, not a guarantee** — worth stating that way in the calculator's content rather than as certainty.
- **Typical default fund equity allocation**: varies significantly by provider and by how close to the stated retirement date the saver is — some default "growth phase" allocations run as high as ~80%+ equities for younger members, tapering down; others are more conservative even in the growth phase (as low as ~40% quoted for some funds). **Actionable point for the calculator**: tell her to actually check what % equities her *specific* Aviva and People's Pension default funds hold today (via her online account/fund factsheet) rather than assuming — allocations differ meaningfully between providers and even between different default fund generations within the same provider.
- **Global equity index funds / OCF**: passive global index tracker funds (e.g. a global all-cap tracker) typically carry an OCF around **0.15–0.23%/year**, versus **0.50–1.5%/year** for actively managed funds. The gap compounds significantly: one illustrative example found in this research shows a 0.7 percentage-point fee difference costing almost £50,000 less in the final pot value over a 25-year period, purely from cost drag on compounding — a concrete, calculator-relevant illustration of why fees matter over long horizons.
  Source: [MoneyWeek – Default pension funds: what's in your workplace pension?](https://moneyweek.com/personal-finance/pensions/workplace-pension-default-pension-funds); [pensionbible.co.uk – Pension Fees Explained 2026/27](https://www.pensionbible.co.uk/guides/pension-fees); [MoneyWeek – OCF glossary](https://moneyweek.com/glossary/ocf-ongoing-charges-figure) **[The specific "£50,000 over 25 years" example is illustrative/aggregated from a secondary source, not a regulator-published figure — safe to reproduce as a worked example if the calculator's own maths independently reconstructs an equivalent illustration, but don't cite it as an official statistic]**

## 8. Pension vs Cash ISA

- **Tax relief vs flexibility trade-off**: pension contributions get upfront tax relief (effectively free money added by the government/employer, see sections 2–3) but the money is locked up until at least the Normal Minimum Pension Age (55, rising to 57 in April 2028); ISA contributions get **no** upfront tax relief but can be accessed at any time, tax-free on the way out, with no age restriction. For someone weighing "put spare business cash into a pension or a Cash ISA," the pension's tax relief (personal or, better, employer contribution — see section 3) is very hard to beat *if the money genuinely isn't needed before retirement age*; the ISA wins on flexibility/access.
- **ISA allowance 2026/27: £20,000** — confirmed directly from gov.uk, unchanged from prior years, and can be split across ISA types (Cash, Stocks & Shares, Lifetime, etc.) up to that combined total.
  Source: [gov.uk – Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs)](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts)
- **Lifetime ISA (LISA) rules**: available to those aged 18–39 when first opened (must be under 40 to open one); annual contribution limit **£4,000**, which counts within the overall £20,000 ISA allowance; government adds a **25% bonus** (so £4,000 in → £5,000 total). Can be used towards a first home (up to £450,000 purchase price) or accessed penalty-free from age 60, or on terminal illness. Withdrawing for any other reason triggers a **25% government withdrawal penalty on the whole withdrawn amount** — because the 25% bonus effectively made up 20% of the balance, this penalty claws back the government bonus *and* costs the saver roughly 6.25% of their own original money, a well-documented "penalty trap." Note: the government announced in the Autumn 2025 Budget that the LISA will be replaced by a new first-time-buyer ISA from around April 2028, though existing LISA holders can reportedly keep contributing in the meantime. Given she's a 40-something (per context), she is likely **already over the LISA age limit to open a new one** — worth checking her exact age before featuring LISA prominently.
  Source: [gov.uk – Lifetime ISA: Overview](https://www.gov.uk/lifetime-isa); [smartinvestoruk.co.uk – Lifetime ISA Rules 2026](https://smartinvestoruk.co.uk/lifetime-isa-rules/); [tembomoney.com – Government issues major update on Lifetime ISA](https://www.tembomoney.com/learn/government-issues-major-update-on-lifetime-isa)
- **Emergency fund guidance**: standard MoneyHelper/consumer-finance guidance is to hold **3–6 months of essential expenses** in an easily accessible cash account before investing/locking money away — particularly important to state explicitly for a self-employed person with variable income, where the case for a larger cash buffer (towards the 6-month end, or higher, given business income volatility) is stronger than for someone on a stable salary. **[General guidance widely repeated across MoneyHelper-style consumer content; a single definitive MoneyHelper page stating exactly "3-6 months" was not pinned down this session — recommend confirming exact MoneyHelper wording before quoting it as their guidance verbatim]**
- **Standard priority order** (widely used personal-finance sequencing, consistent with MoneyHelper-style guidance): (1) build a 3–6 month emergency cash fund → (2) contribute enough to get any available employer pension match (free money) → (3) clear high-interest debt (credit cards etc.) → (4) maximise tax-advantaged pension contributions (especially valuable for a director via employer contributions, see section 3) → (5) use remaining ISA allowance for further tax-free flexible saving. This ordering is a well-established convention across UK financial guidance content rather than a single official published "rule," but it's a fair, uncontroversial sequence to present.

## 9. Regulatory line — what a non-advised calculator must respect (shapes chatbot guardrails)

- **Regulated advice vs guidance — the core legal test (FCA Handbook PERG 8.30B, "personal recommendation")**: a communication is a regulated **personal recommendation** (requiring FCA authorisation) when it is (a) addressed to someone as an investor/potential investor, (b) about a particular investment, (c) either presented as suitable for that person OR based on a consideration of that person's actual circumstances, and (d) not issued exclusively to the public at large. Concretely:
  - **Crosses into advice**: language like *"this would be the best option for you"*, or implicitly steering one specific option toward a specific person based on their stated circumstances.
  - **Stays as guidance/information**: filtering or displaying options by category (e.g. by risk level) without endorsing which one suits the individual; general factual explanations of how pension rules work; generic educational content published to anyone visiting the page.
  - **Important nuance directly relevant to a calculator**: FCA commentary is explicit that **a disclaimer alone does not fix advice-like content** — "a legalistic disclaimer is unlikely to be enough to prevent a firm from giving a personal recommendation; instead, the material should prominently and clearly explain the limited nature of the service." In other words, if the calculator's actual output reads like "you should do X," slapping a "this isn't advice" label on it does not make it compliant — the *substance* of the output has to stay general and non-personalised, not just carry a caveat.
  Source: [FCA Handbook – PERG 8.30B, Personal recommendations](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/perg8/perg8s41); [Pinsent Masons – FCA clarifies advice-guidance difference](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/fca-clarifies-advice-guidance-difference-amidst-ongoing-review)
- **Important 2026 regulatory development — "Targeted Support"**: the FCA's Advice Guidance Boundary Review has created a **new, intermediate regime called "Targeted Support"**, sitting between pure guidance and full regulated advice. The FCA published near-final rules on 11 December 2025, the FCA Board confirmed final rules on 26 February 2026, and the new regime is being rolled out from **early April 2026** (authorisation gateway open from March 2026). Targeted Support allows *authorised* firms to give suggestions to groups of customers who share common characteristics (e.g. "people like you in your situation typically do X") without it being a full personalised recommendation. **This is directly relevant**: if a non-advised calculator strays from pure generic guidance/information into anything that looks like "people like you should do X," it risks straddling into Targeted Support/advice territory, which requires specific FCA permissions the calculator's operator likely does not (and should not, without proper authorisation) claim to have. Keep the calculator firmly in "here's the maths and general information" territory, not "here's what you personally should do."
  Source: [FCA – Advice Guidance Boundary Review](https://www.fca.org.uk/firms/advice-guidance-boundary-review); [Hogan Lovells – Targeted support update, FCA publishes near-final rules](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/targeted-support-update-fca-publishes-nearfinal-rules-on-new-form-of-advice); [Farrer & Co – The Advice Guidance Boundary Review: the new targeted support regime](https://www.farrer.co.uk/news-and-insights/the-advice-guidance-boundary-review-the-new-targeted-support-regime/)
- **Concrete disclaimer wording to use** (drawing on the PERG guidance above plus a documented real-world example format — "The Pensions Office is not authorised or regulated to provide financial or other advice. The information provided is not intended to be and does not constitute financial advice or any other advice." — adapted for this product):
  1. State plainly, near the top of any results/output screen, not just buried in a footer: **"This tool gives general information and illustrative projections only. It is not personalised financial advice and does not take into account your full personal circumstances. [Product name] is not authorised or regulated by the FCA to give financial advice."**
  2. State the numbers are estimates: **"These figures are estimates based on the assumptions shown (growth rate, inflation, and the contributions you've entered). Actual outcomes will differ and are not guaranteed."**
  3. Actively signpost rather than just disclaim: **"For guidance on your options, free and impartial help is available from MoneyHelper (moneyhelper.org.uk) and, if you're 50 or over, Pension Wise. For advice on a specific decision — such as transferring a pension or how to take your retirement income — speak to a regulated financial adviser."**
  4. Content/wording rule for whoever writes the copy: avoid "you should," "we recommend," or presenting one path as the right one *for this specific user*; phrase things as "many people in this situation consider..." or "one option is..." — i.e., keep it general, not a recommendation tailored to her.

## 10. Assumption conventions for illustrations

- **FCA COBS projection rate (regulated illustrations)**: for projections used in FCA-regulated "key features illustrations," the future inflation adjustment used is **2% per year** (per COBS rules referenced in industry commentary). **[Confidence: moderate — sourced from secondary industry commentary rather than fetched directly from the FCA Handbook COBS section this session; recommend confirming the exact current COBS provision/rate before quoting authoritatively]**
- **SMPI (Statutory Money Purchase Illustration) growth rate assumptions**: since a Financial Reporting Council (FRC) methodology change effective **1 October 2023**, SMPIs (the annual illustration your pension provider sends, e.g. from Aviva/People's Pension) use one of **four standardised future growth rates — 1%, 3%, 5%, or 7%** per year — with the specific rate assigned per fund based on that fund's own volatility over the preceding 5 years (rather than a single flat assumption for all funds as before). This means the "growth rate" shown on her actual Aviva/People's Pension annual statements is fund-specific and standardised across the industry for comparability.
  Source: [Standard Life Employer – A guide to FRC changes to SMPI](https://www.standardlife.co.uk/employer/guides-and-articles/changes-to-smpi); [investcentre.co.uk – Statutory money purchase illustrations](https://www.investcentre.co.uk/articles/statutory-money-purchase-illustrations)
- **SMPI inflation assumption: 2.5%** per year (set by the FRC), distinct from the COBS 2% figure used for other regulated illustrations — worth being precise about which convention the calculator is following if it quotes "the industry standard inflation assumption," since there are two different standard figures in use for different document types.
  Source: [investcentre.co.uk – Statutory money purchase illustrations](https://www.investcentre.co.uk/articles/statutory-money-purchase-illustrations)
- **Why present figures in "today's money" (real terms)**: standard practice (and generally an FCA-encouraged/required approach for illustrations, via inflation-adjusted/"in today's terms" presentation rules introduced via FCA policy work such as PS13/3 on inflation-adjusted illustrations) is to strip out assumed future inflation so the numbers shown are comparable to what things cost *today*, rather than an inflated nominal number that looks big but has less real purchasing power — critical for a non-technical user to actually understand what a projected pot will *buy* rather than being falsely reassured by a large nominal number three decades out.
  Source: [FCA – PS13/3: Personal pensions – inflation-adjusted illustrations](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/fsa-ps13-02.pdf)
